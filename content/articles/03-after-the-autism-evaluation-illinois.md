# After the Autism Evaluation: What Comes Next for Your Child in Illinois

The evaluation is over. The feedback session happened, someone said the word "autism" about your child, and you drove home with a thick report, a list of recommendations, and a strange mix of feelings you may not have words for yet.

I want to start there, actually — with the feelings — because in nearly two decades of working with families, I've learned that what parents need first after a diagnosis is rarely another to-do list. So before the practical steps: whatever you're feeling right now is allowed. Relief, because the struggles finally have a name and it isn't "bad parenting." Grief, for the path you'd imagined. Vindication, if you've been telling doctors and teachers for years that something was different. Guilt about the relief, guilt about the grief. All of it is normal, all of it can coexist, and none of it means you love your child any less.

And one truth to hold onto: **your child is exactly the same person they were the day before the evaluation.** The diagnosis didn't change them. It gave you better information about how to help them thrive.

Now — here's the roadmap I walk families through.

## Step 1: Actually read the report (twice)

Evaluation reports are dense, and most parents read the summary page and stop. Go back when you're ready and read the whole thing, because the middle sections are where the useful material lives: your child's specific profile of strengths and challenges — language, sensory patterns, executive functioning, anxiety, social communication.

Two things to know as you read:

- **"High-functioning" isn't in there for a reason.** Clinicians now describe *support needs* rather than functioning levels, because a child who converses brilliantly may still need substantial support with transitions, friendships, or sensory overload. If your child is verbal, bright, and struggling invisibly, the report's details will validate a lot of what you've seen at home that others miss.
- **The recommendations section is your planning document.** Highlight it. It's written partly *for* the school and *for* therapists — you'll be handing it to both.

If your child's report also mentions giftedness alongside autism, you're in twice-exceptional territory, which comes with its own particular camouflage — I've written about that here: [link: →2].

## Step 2: Bring the school in

In Illinois, an outside (private) evaluation doesn't automatically trigger school services — you have to ask. Send a written request to your school's principal or special education coordinator asking for an evaluation for eligibility, and attach the private report. Two main paths exist:

- **An IEP** (Individualized Education Program) if your child qualifies for special education services, or
- **A 504 Plan** if they don't need specialized instruction but do need accommodations — extended time, sensory breaks, seating changes, warning before transitions.

Bright autistic kids with strong grades are sometimes told they "don't need anything" because their academics look fine. Grades are not the only criterion — social, emotional, and functional needs count. The private evaluation report is your strongest tool in that conversation, and you're entitled to disagree with the school's conclusions and pursue it further.

## Step 3: Choose supports deliberately — not maximally

The recommendations page can read like a part-time job: OT, speech, social skills group, counseling, parent training. Families burn out trying to do everything at once, and the child feels like their whole life became appointments.

My advice is always the same: **pick the one or two things that address the loudest current struggle, start there, and add later.** If anxiety and meltdowns are dominating your home, start with counseling. If sensory issues are blocking everything else, start with OT. A calmer child and a steadier family make every subsequent support work better.

And guard your child's joy. Whatever they love — dinosaurs, coding, drawing, swimming — is not a reward to be earned or a slot to be sacrificed for another therapy hour. Their interests are where confidence and identity live. Protect that time like it's a prescription, because it is.

## Step 4: Get support for the emotional side — theirs and yours

This is my corner of the work, so let me be specific about what counseling does and doesn't do here.

Counseling for an autistic child isn't about making them less autistic. In my practice, it's about the things that make life harder than it needs to be: the anxiety that so often travels with autism, the big emotions that arrive faster than the tools to handle them, the exhausting work of masking all day at school, the self-esteem question every perceptive autistic kid eventually asks — *why am I different?*

With younger children I work through play, because play is where kids process what they can't yet say. With older kids and teens, we work directly and honestly — autistic kids tend to deeply appreciate an adult who is concrete, genuine, and doesn't make them decode social subtext in therapy of all places.

And I work with *parents*, always. An attachment-focused approach means the relationship between you and your child is the primary instrument of change: understanding what a meltdown actually is (a nervous system past capacity, not defiance), learning what helps your particular child regulate, and — just as important — having a place to process your own experience. If the diagnosis is stirring up more than you can carry, that's not weakness; parenting is relational work, and you deserve support in it too.

One practical note: many autistic kids do beautifully in telehealth — home is their regulated space, there's no waiting room, no fluorescent lights, no transition to manage. I see families this way across all of Illinois. (More on how virtual sessions work: [link: →5].)

## Step 5: Connect — carefully — with the community

Other parents a few years ahead of you are an extraordinary resource for the Illinois-specific practicalities: which districts do what, which providers are worth the waitlist. Look for local parent groups and autism organizations — and, as your child grows, let them encounter autistic teens and adults who are living full, particular, genuinely good lives. The most powerful thing a newly diagnosed kid can learn is that they're not a broken version of normal; they're a normal version of themselves.

## The unhurried truth

Nothing on this list expires. You don't have to do it all this month. The diagnosis gave you a map, not a deadline — and the fact that you sought answers at all means your child already has the thing that matters most: a parent paying real attention.
