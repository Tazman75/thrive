# Does Insurance Cover My Child's Therapy? Aetna & BCBS Illinois, Explained

Here's a secret from the other side of the couch: for a lot of families, the hardest part of starting therapy isn't the therapy. It's the insurance. Parents put off getting their child help for months — not because they're unsure about counseling, but because they're dreading the maze of copays, deductibles, networks, and phone trees.

So let me walk you through it in plain English. ## The short version

- **Yes, health insurance covers therapy for children and teens.** Mental health care is an essential health benefit, and federal parity law requires plans to cover mental health comparably to medical care. Therapy with a licensed counselor for anxiety, depression, ADHD-related struggles, autism-related support, and family concerns is standard covered care — not a fringe benefit.
- **Yes, that includes telehealth.** Illinois law requires state-regulated health plans to cover care delivered via telehealth, and both Aetna and BCBS IL routinely cover video therapy sessions. My practice is fully telehealth, serving families across Illinois.
- **What you'll actually pay** depends on your specific plan — usually either a flat copay per session or a portion of the cost until you hit your deductible. Here's how to decode that.

## The four words that decide what you pay

**Copay.** A fixed amount you pay per session — often somewhere in the $20–50 range for outpatient mental health, though every plan differs. If your plan uses copays for therapy, budgeting is easy: that number, per session, and insurance handles the rest.

**Deductible.** The amount you pay out of pocket each year before your insurance starts sharing costs. If your plan applies therapy to the deductible and you haven't met it yet, you'll pay the insurance-negotiated session rate (typically well below a therapist's private-pay rate) until you do.

**Coinsurance.** After the deductible is met, some plans switch to a percentage split — you pay, say, 20% of the negotiated rate and insurance pays the rest.

**Out-of-pocket maximum.** The annual ceiling on what your family pays in total. After you hit it, covered care — including therapy — is 100% paid. Families who have a big medical year sometimes realize mid-fall that therapy is effectively free for the rest of the year.

## Common questions from Illinois parents

**Do I need a referral from our pediatrician?**
With most PPO plans, no — you can book directly with a therapist. Some HMO plans do require a referral or have their own network rules; the booking process will confirm your plan's coverage either way, so you'll know before committing to anything.

**Does my child need a diagnosis for insurance to pay?**
Insurance does require a clinical diagnosis to cover treatment — that's how medical billing works generally, not something unique to mental health. Common diagnoses like an anxiety disorder or adjustment disorder qualify. This is a normal part of the process, it's confidential, and I'm always glad to talk with parents about what a diagnosis does and doesn't mean for their child.

**Is telehealth therapy covered the same as in-office?**
For Aetna and BCBS IL plans I work with, video sessions are covered outpatient care. Illinois has required telehealth coverage in state-regulated plans since 2021 — video therapy stopped being the exception years ago. (Wondering whether virtual sessions actually *work* for kids and teens? I wrote about that here: [link: →5].)

**What if we have a different insurance company?**
Two options. If your plan has out-of-network benefits, I can provide a **superbill** — a detailed receipt you submit for partial reimbursement; many PPO plans reimburse a meaningful share of the cost. Or you can simply self-pay at **$130 per session**, which some families prefer for privacy or simplicity. And it's worth checking back — I expect to add more insurance plans over time.

**How many sessions will insurance cover?**
Modern plans generally don't impose a fixed session cap on outpatient therapy; coverage continues while care is medically appropriate. We'll always be working toward your child's goals, not an insurance clock.
