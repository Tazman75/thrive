# Online Therapy for Teens in Illinois: Does Telehealth Actually Work for Teenagers?

Let me guess the conversation that brought you here. You've been worried about your teenager for a while — the anxiety, the shut bedroom door, the grades slipping or the spark dimming — and you finally floated the idea of therapy. And your teen said some version of: *"Fine. But I'm not going to some office."*

Or maybe the skeptic is you. Video calls for work meetings, sure — but for something as personal as your kid's mental health? Can a therapist really connect with a fifteen-year-old through a screen?

I've been counseling adolescents for nearly two decades, and my practice today is fully telehealth, serving teens across Illinois. So I'll give you my honest answer — including where telehealth *isn't* the right fit, because that's part of an honest answer too.

## What the research says (briefly)

Study after study comparing video-delivered therapy to in-person therapy has reached the same broad conclusion: for the most common concerns that bring teens to counseling — anxiety, depression, stress, school struggles — **outcomes are comparable**. The evidence became especially deep after 2020, when essentially the entire field moved online at once and researchers could study teletherapy at scale. The consistent finding: the *relationship* between therapist and client predicts success, and that relationship forms just as well over video as across a room.

## Many teens are actually *better* in online therapy

This surprises parents, but it shouldn't. Consider the setup from your teen's side:

**Their room is their territory.** A therapist's office is adult turf — unfamiliar furniture, a stranger's diplomas, a parent in the waiting room. On video, your teen is in their own space with their headphones and their cat. Teens who'd spend three in-person sessions warming up often skip that stage entirely at home.

**The screen lowers the intensity.** Sustained face-to-face eye contact with an adult while discussing your feelings is, for many teens — especially anxious or neurodivergent ones — the *hardest possible format*. A screen softens that. Some of my most guarded clients talk most freely while doodling, fidgeting, or looking just off-camera. That's not disengagement; that's a nervous system comfortable enough to speak.

**It's native, not novel.** This generation has conducted friendships, arguments, and confessions over video and voice chat their whole lives. For them, meaningful conversation through a screen isn't a degraded copy of "real" connection — it *is* real connection. The awkward one in the room is us, not them.

**No logistics tax.** No leaving practice early, no traffic on a school night, no "whose week is it to drive." A session slots into a Tuesday evening without disrupting the schedule — which means therapy actually *continues* through busy seasons instead of quietly falling off the calendar. Consistency is half of what makes therapy work.

## What a virtual session actually looks like

Practically: sessions happen over a secure, HIPAA-compliant video platform — not FaceTime, not Zoom-with-fingers-crossed. Your teen needs a device, decent wifi, and a private space; a bedroom with the door closed works, and headphones handle most privacy concerns beyond that.

The session itself looks like therapy, because it is. We talk, but not in a stiff interview — with younger teens I use games, drawing, music, and shared screens; with older teens it's often closer to a real working conversation about what's actually going on. Same skills, same evidence-based approaches, same goals as any office session.

**And parents aren't shut out.** With minors, I keep parents genuinely involved — check-ins, family sessions when useful, and clear agreements everyone understands about what stays private between your teen and me and what doesn't. (Safety concerns always reach parents. Teens know this from day one; clarity is what makes them feel safe enough to be honest.)

## Where telehealth is NOT the right fit — the honest part

I'd rather you hear this from a therapist than discover it mid-crisis. Online therapy is not the best setting when:

- **Safety is an active concern** — a teen in crisis, self-harming, or having suicidal thoughts needs a higher, more immediate level of care than weekly video sessions, and often in-person support.
- **There's truly no private space** — if the only option is whispering within earshot of siblings, the work suffers. (Though families are resourceful: parked cars in the driveway have hosted some excellent sessions.)
- **Your teen flatly refuses the format** — rare, in my experience, since teens usually prefer video, but a teen with zero buy-in to either format needs the buy-in conversation first, and I often start that work with the parents.

If any of the safety flags apply to your family, call or text **988** (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or go to your nearest emergency room. That comes before any scheduling decision.

## The bottom line for a skeptical parent

The format matters far less than the fit. A teen who connects with their therapist over video will outgain a teen dragged to a beige office twice a month, every time. If online therapy is the version your teenager will actually show up for — willingly, weekly, from their own room — then online therapy is the version that works.
