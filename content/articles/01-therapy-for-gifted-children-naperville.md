# Therapy for Gifted Children in Naperville: Why Bright Kids Struggle — and How Counseling Helps

"But she's so smart. Why is she falling apart over homework she could do in her sleep?"

If you're the parent of a gifted child, some version of that question has probably kept you up at night. Maybe it's the straight-A student who dissolves into tears over a single wrong answer. The nine-year-old who debates like a lawyer but can't handle losing a board game. The kid who reads three grade levels ahead and still refuses to go to school on Mondays.

I've spent nearly two decades counseling children, teens, and families, and a large part of my practice is gifted and twice-exceptional kids — many of them right here in the Naperville area, where strong gifted programs in our local school districts mean a lot of families are living this exact experience. Here's what I want you to know: your child isn't broken, and you're not imagining it. Giftedness genuinely changes how a child experiences the world — and sometimes that experience is overwhelming.

## Why gifted kids struggle (even when everything looks fine on paper)

### Their minds and emotions grow at different speeds

Psychologists call this **asynchronous development**: a child can be twelve intellectually, eight emotionally, and ten socially — all at once, in the same small body. A gifted child may understand climate change, mortality, or injustice years before they have the emotional equipment to cope with that understanding. The result often looks like anxiety, existential worry, or meltdowns that seem to come out of nowhere.

### Perfectionism sets in early — and it's sneaky

Many gifted kids sail through their early years without ever having to struggle. Then, when something finally *is* hard — a math concept, a friendship, a tryout — they have no experience with productive struggle, and it feels catastrophic. Perfectionism in gifted children rarely looks like neat handwriting. It looks like:

- Refusing to start assignments they can't do perfectly
- Tearing up work and starting over, again and again
- Avoiding new activities where they might not immediately excel
- Harsh self-talk that would break your heart if you heard it out loud

### They feel everything more intensely

Gifted children often experience emotions, sensations, and imagination with unusual intensity — a big reaction isn't drama, it's genuinely how loud the world is for them. A tag in a shirt, a change in plans, an "unfair" call at recess: what looks like overreaction is often a nervous system running at a higher volume.

### They can feel out of sync with their peers

When your inner world runs on a different track from your classmates', friendship gets complicated. Some gifted kids gravitate toward adults or older children; some mask their abilities to fit in; some simply feel lonely without being able to name why. That "out of sync" feeling is one of the most common threads I see in my work with gifted kids.

## When it's time to consider counseling

Every child has hard weeks. It's worth reaching out when the hard weeks become the pattern:

- School refusal or Sunday-night dread that doesn't fade
- Meltdowns that are intense, frequent, and hard to recover from
- Sleep problems, stomachaches, or headaches with no medical cause
- A confident kid who has gone quiet, angry, or self-critical
- Perfectionism that's stopping them from trying things at all
- You've started walking on eggshells at home

## What therapy with a gifted child actually looks like

Gifted kids tend to be therapy-savvy in the funniest way — they'll spot a canned worksheet from across the room. So my work with them doesn't look like a lecture about feelings. It looks like:

**Play and creative expression.** For younger children, play therapy is the native language — it lets a child work through what they can't yet say. And because many gifted kids are also creative kids, art, story, and music often become part of how we work. (As a lifelong musician and dancer myself, I never underestimate what the arts can carry.)

**Attachment-focused family work.** A gifted child's struggles never happen in a vacuum. I work with parents as genuine partners — not to blame, but to equip. Often the most powerful change happens when parents understand *why* their child melts down and learn responses that calm rather than escalate.

**Real skills, honestly taught.** Gifted kids can understand the "why" behind a coping strategy, so I teach it to them straight: what anxiety is doing in their body, why avoidance feeds it, how to argue back at perfectionist thoughts. When a bright kid understands the mechanism, they use the tool.

**Respect for the whole child.** Giftedness is not a problem to be fixed. The goal of therapy is never to sand down intensity or curiosity — it's to help your child carry their own mind more comfortably.
