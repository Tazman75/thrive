# Anxiety, ADHD, or Both? A Parent's Guide to Twice-Exceptional (2e) Kids

There's a particular kind of parent exhaustion I recognize the moment a family sits down with me. It's the exhaustion of contradiction: *my child tests in the 99th percentile and is failing math. My child can hyperfocus on Minecraft for four hours and can't get through a five-minute worksheet. My child's teacher says everything is fine, and every night at home is a war.*

If that's your house, you may be raising a **twice-exceptional (2e) child** — a child who is gifted *and* has a learning, attention, or emotional difference such as ADHD, anxiety, autism, or dyslexia. In nearly two decades of counseling children and families, 2e kids have become one of the populations I care most about, because they are so often missed, mislabeled, or told they're simply not trying.

## Why 2e kids are so hard to spot

Giftedness and challenges like ADHD or anxiety don't sit side by side — they *camouflage each other*.

**The strengths hide the struggle.** A gifted child with ADHD can often compensate through sheer intellect. They hold it together at school (barely, at enormous cost) and get grades just good enough that no one investigates. Teachers see "fine." You see the 4 p.m. collapse.

**The struggle hides the strengths.** In other kids, it flips: the inattention, the anxiety, the unfinished work drag their performance down to "average," so no one sees the gifted mind underneath. These kids often stop being challenged at all — which makes everything worse.

**And the labels blur.** The traits genuinely overlap. Which brings us to the question parents ask me most.

## Anxiety, ADHD, or "just gifted"? How the three get confused

The same visible behavior can have three different engines underneath:

**A child who can't sit still and interrupts constantly** might have ADHD — or might be a gifted child who finished the assignment ten minutes ago and is bored beyond endurance. The tell is context: ADHD shows up *everywhere*, including during activities the child loves. Boredom-driven restlessness disappears the moment the material gets interesting.

**A child who won't start their work** might have ADHD-related executive-functioning trouble (they genuinely can't organize the steps to begin) — or anxiety-driven perfectionism (they can't tolerate starting something they might do wrong). These look identical from the kitchen doorway. They need very different help.

**A child who "doesn't listen"** might have attention differences — or might be so tangled in anxious what-ifs that there's no bandwidth left for your voice. Anxious kids are often mislabeled as inattentive; the attention isn't absent, it's *hijacked*.

**And often, honestly, it's both.** Anxiety and ADHD co-occur frequently, and in gifted kids the anxiety is often *about* the ADHD — the bright child knows exactly what they should be able to do, watches themselves fail at it, and concludes something is deeply wrong with them. That gap between "what I know I can do" and "what I can actually produce" is, in my experience, the single most painful thing about being 2e.

## Signs your gifted child may be twice-exceptional

No checklist replaces an evaluation, but these patterns are worth taking seriously:

- Big, persistent gaps — brilliant verbally but can't get thoughts on paper; advanced reader, unfinished everything
- Performance that swings wildly by subject, teacher, or day
- Hours of homework that should take twenty minutes
- Intense frustration or shame about their own abilities ("I'm so stupid" from a child you know is anything but)
- School reports that don't match the child you see at home — in either direction
- Sleep problems, stomachaches, or dread around school
- A child who works twice as hard as peers to appear "normal" — and falls apart after holding it together all day

## What actually helps

**1. Get a real picture first.** If you haven't had a comprehensive evaluation (psychoeducational or neuropsychological testing), that's often the right first step — it separates the strands in a way observation alone can't. If you've just come through an evaluation and are staring at a report wondering *now what*, I wrote a guide for exactly that moment: [link: →3].

**2. Treat the whole child, not the label.** In counseling with 2e kids, I work on the anxiety and the executive struggles *and* the relationship the child has with their own mind. A 2e child who understands their brain — what it's great at, where it needs scaffolding, and that the gap isn't a character flaw — is transformed by that understanding. Because these kids are conceptual thinkers, I teach them the actual mechanics: what anxiety does in the body, why avoidance grows it, how working memory affects starting tasks. Bright kids use tools they understand.

**3. Bring the parents in.** So much of the daily friction in 2e households — homework battles, morning chaos, the eggshell feeling — improves when parents can tell the difference between *won't* and *can't* in the moment. That's a skill, and it's teachable. My approach is attachment-focused: the goal isn't a better-behaved child, it's a child who feels safe enough with you to stop masking.

**4. Protect the strengths.** A 2e child whose whole life becomes remediation — tutors, therapies, interventions — loses the thing that fuels them. Whatever your child loves and excels at needs guarded time, because that's where their confidence lives. This is a treatment principle, not a nice-to-have. (I say more about how giftedness itself shapes a child's inner life here: [link: →1].)

## You're not imagining it

If one message lands from this article, let it be this: a child can be genuinely gifted and genuinely struggling at the same time, and needing help with one doesn't erase the other. The parents who find me are often carrying years of conflicting feedback — *she's fine, he's lazy, she just needs to apply herself*. You know your child. The contradiction you're seeing is real, it has a name, and it responds to the right kind of support.
